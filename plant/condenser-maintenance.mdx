The condenser is the heat rejection component of every refrigeration and air conditioning system — it transfers heat absorbed from the refrigerated or conditioned space into either ambient air, a water circuit, or a combined air-and-water evaporative process. A poorly maintained condenser raises condensing pressure, increases compressor power consumption, reduces capacity, and ultimately shortens equipment life. The maintenance requirements vary significantly between the three principal condenser types: **air-cooled finned coil**, **water-cooled shell-and-tube or brazed plate**, and **evaporative**. Follow the procedures for the condenser type installed on your system.

<Tip>
  Schedule your annual condenser deep clean **4–6 weeks before the expected start of peak cooling season** (typically late spring in temperate climates). Carrying out the clean before the hottest weather arrives ensures the system enters its highest-demand period with maximum heat rejection capacity, minimising the risk of high-pressure trips and reduced efficiency during peak load.
</Tip>

<Note>
  Water-cooled and evaporative condenser water treatment should be managed by a qualified HVAC water treatment specialist. Poorly managed water chemistry causes accelerated corrosion, scale fouling, and biological contamination that can render equipment unserviceable within a single season. Engage a specialist to set up a dosing programme, provide test kits, and carry out periodic audits of water quality.
</Note>

## Maintenance Procedures by Condenser Type

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Air-Cooled Condenser">
    Air-cooled condensers use a finned coil through which refrigerant vapour flows, with one or more fans drawing or forcing ambient air across the fin surface to reject heat. They are the most common condenser type in light commercial and medium commercial refrigeration systems. Their primary maintenance requirement is keeping the fin and coil surface clean and the fans operating correctly.

    ### Monthly Checks

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Inspect Fins for Damage, Dirt, and Debris">
        Visually inspect the entire face of the condenser coil from both the air inlet and air discharge sides. Look for: flattened or bent fins restricting airflow, accumulations of dust, grease, cottonwood seeds, or other airborne debris blocking the fin array, and any physical impact damage to the coil face. Note the degree of fouling — a heavily fouled coil face will be visible as a matted layer across the fins. Record findings and proceed with cleaning if fouling is present.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Clean the Condenser Coil">
        Where fouling is moderate to heavy, clean the coil using an approved, non-acidic foaming coil cleaner suitable for aluminium fin and copper tube construction:
        1. Isolate and lock off condenser fans before cleaning.
        2. Apply foaming coil cleaner with a hand pump sprayer, working from the air inlet face. Allow the foam to penetrate and dwell for the time specified by the cleaner manufacturer.
        3. Rinse the coil thoroughly with a low-pressure water hose, flushing from the clean (discharge) side through to the inlet face to push loosened debris out in the direction it entered.
        4. Do not use high-pressure water jets — fin arrays are fragile and high-pressure impingement bends fins and reduces airflow.
        5. Allow the coil to drain fully before restoring fan power.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Condenser Fan Amp Draw and Rotation Direction">
        With the system running, use a clamp meter to check the amp draw of each condenser fan motor against its nameplate full-load amperage (FLA). An amp draw significantly above FLA indicates a motor fault, worn bearings, or incorrect capacitor; significantly below FLA on a PSC motor may indicate a failing run capacitor. Confirm fan blade rotation direction produces airflow in the direction indicated by the unit manufacturer (typically air across coil, exhausted upward or outward). Reversed rotation results in dramatically reduced airflow and elevated condensing pressure.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Refrigerant Condensing Temperature and Pressure">
        Read the discharge pressure on the system gauge set and convert to condensing saturation temperature using a pressure–temperature chart for the refrigerant in use. Compare to the ambient air temperature: the design condensing temperature is typically **ambient + 10–15°C** for air-cooled condensers. A condensing temperature that is excessively high relative to ambient (more than 15–20°C above ambient) indicates either a dirty or airflow-restricted coil, a fan failure, or refrigerant overcharge. Investigate and correct before the fault leads to high-pressure trips.
      </Step>
    </Steps>

    ### Annual Service

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Deep Chemical Clean">
        Carry out a thorough chemical clean using a suitable coil cleaner — for heavily fouled coils in food service or industrial environments, a stronger alkaline or mildly acidic cleaner (following the manufacturer's safety guidance and material compatibility recommendations) may be required. Apply, dwell, and rinse as per the monthly procedure, but allow additional dwell time and repeat the application if fouling is heavy. Inspect the coil face after cleaning under good lighting.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Straighten Bent Fins with Fin Comb">
        Using a fin comb with the correct fin pitch for the coil, carefully straighten any bent or crushed fin sections across the full coil face. Restored fin spacing significantly improves airflow and heat transfer. Work methodically across the coil face in the direction of the fins. Do not force the comb — use light, consistent strokes.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Coil for Refrigerant Leaks">
        Using a calibrated electronic refrigerant sniffer, systematically probe the entire coil assembly including the return bends, headers, and distributor connections. Coil leaks in air-cooled condensers can develop from vibration fatigue, corrosion (particularly in coastal or high-pollution environments), or mechanical damage. If a leak is detected, document the location and arrange for repair or coil replacement.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Fan Blades for Cracks and Damage">
        With power isolated and locked off, manually inspect each fan blade on all condenser fan assemblies. Look for hairline cracks at the blade root or hub, impact chips, deformation, or erosion at the blade tips. A cracked fan blade is a safety hazard — replace the fan assembly immediately if cracks are found. Check that all blade tip clearances are uniform (uneven clearances indicate a bent blade or a bent shaft).
      </Step>
      <Step title="Replace Worn Fan Motor Capacitors">
        PSC (permanent split capacitor) condenser fan motors rely on a run capacitor for correct operation. Capacitors degrade over time, causing the motor to draw excessive current, run hot, and deliver reduced output. At the annual service, measure the capacitance of each run capacitor using a capacitance meter and compare to the nameplate value. Replace any capacitor that has drifted more than ±6% from its rated value, or any capacitor showing physical signs of bulging or leakage. Run capacitors are low-cost consumables; proactive replacement prevents motor burnout.
      </Step>
    </Steps>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Water-Cooled Condenser">
    Water-cooled condensers — most commonly shell-and-tube or brazed-plate heat exchangers — reject refrigerant heat into a recirculating water circuit, which in turn is cooled by a cooling tower, dry cooler, or mains water supply. They offer more consistent heat rejection than air-cooled units but require active water treatment management to prevent scale, corrosion, and biological fouling.

    ### Monthly Checks

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Check Condenser Water Inlet and Outlet Temperatures">
        Using the system's temperature sensors or a calibrated contact thermometer, record the condenser water inlet and outlet temperatures. The design temperature rise across the condenser is typically **5–6°C** (i.e., water enters at 30°C and leaves at 35–36°C at design conditions). A higher-than-design ΔT indicates reduced water flow rate or internal fouling increasing thermal resistance. A lower-than-design ΔT may indicate higher-than-design flow rate. Log the values and compare against previous readings to identify trends.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Verify Condenser Water Flow Rate and Strainer Differential Pressure">
        Check the condenser water pump is running and confirm the flow rate is within design specification (check the pump duty on the commissioning sheet). Inspect the water-side strainer: measure differential pressure across the strainer if gauges are fitted, or close the isolating valves and open the blow-down to inspect the strainer basket for debris. A blocked strainer basket is a common cause of reduced flow rate and elevated condensing pressure. Clean the basket and note debris type — large quantities of scale or biofilm indicate a water treatment problem.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Condenser Water Treatment Parameters">
        Test the condenser water circuit using appropriate test strips or a digital water analysis kit. Record and compare to target ranges:
        - **pH**: 7.0–8.5 (outside this range accelerates corrosion of copper tubes and steel headers)
        - **Conductivity**: within the limits set by your water treatment specialist (typically 1,000–3,000 µS/cm depending on local mains water hardness)
        - **Biocide concentration**: confirm dosing pump is operating and the biocide residual is within the effective treatment range
        - **Corrosion inhibitor**: confirm inhibitor levels are maintained per the treatment programme

        Forward results to your water treatment specialist if any parameter is out of range. Do not adjust dosing without specialist guidance.
      </Step>
    </Steps>

    ### Annual Service

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Tube Cleaning — Mechanical or Hydro-Blast">
        Scale and fouling on the internal surfaces of condenser tubes is the single greatest cause of reduced heat transfer and elevated condensing pressure in water-cooled systems. At the annual service, isolate the condenser water circuit, drain the waterside, and carry out tube cleaning:
        - **Mechanical brush cleaning**: pass a nylon tube brush of the correct diameter through each tube from both ends. This is effective for soft scale and biofilm.
        - **High-pressure hydro-blasting**: use a professional hydro-blasting rig to flush each tube with a high-velocity water jet, removing hard scale deposits that brushes cannot dislodge.

        After cleaning, flush the waterside with clean water and inspect for tube cleanliness before reassembly.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Tube Sheet, Header, and Sacrificial Anodes">
        With the condenser waterside heads removed, inspect the tube sheet (the plate through which the tubes are rolled or expanded) for signs of corrosion, erosion at tube entries, or tube-to-tube sheet joint weeping. Inspect the waterside header itself for pitting corrosion or crevice corrosion at bolted joints. Check and measure the sacrificial anodes (zinc or magnesium, where fitted) — replace any anode that has been consumed by more than 50% of its original mass. Anodes protect the heat exchanger from galvanic corrosion and are a planned consumable.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Hydraulic Pressure Test (If Waterside is Opened)">
        Whenever the condenser waterside is opened for tube cleaning or inspection, carry out a hydraulic pressure test before returning to service. Close all waterside isolations, fill with clean water, bleed air, and pressurise to 1.5× the system working pressure (or as specified by the manufacturer). Hold for a minimum of 30 minutes and verify no pressure drop. Inspect all joints and connections for weeping. Record the test pressure, duration, and result in the service record.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Water Flow Regulating Valve Operation">
        Many water-cooled systems use a water regulating valve (WRV) that modulates condenser water flow in response to refrigerant condensing pressure, maintaining condensing pressure above a minimum threshold during low-load conditions. Verify the WRV opens and closes smoothly across its operating range and that it fully opens under design load conditions. A sticking or failed WRV causes either excessive condensing pressure (valve stuck closed) or excessively low condensing pressure at part load (valve stuck open). Replace valve internals or the full valve body if operation is not smooth.
      </Step>
    </Steps>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Evaporative Condenser">
    Evaporative condensers combine water evaporation and forced airflow to reject heat from the refrigerant coil, achieving lower condensing temperatures than air-cooled units. They are used on medium to large commercial and industrial refrigeration systems. Their water circuit and wetted surfaces require vigilant water treatment and Legionella management in addition to standard HVAC maintenance.

    <Warning>
      Evaporative condensers present a **Legionella risk** because they maintain warm water (typically 25–40°C) in a sump, circulate that water through spray nozzles, and produce an aerosol that can travel beyond the plant room. Under the **Health and Safety at Work Act 1974** and the **ACOP L8 (Legionnaires' Disease: The Control of Legionella Bacteria in Water Systems)**, you have a legal duty to assess, manage, and control Legionella risk in evaporative condensers. This requires a written Legionella risk assessment, a documented water treatment scheme, and regular water sampling and analysis. Engage a competent water treatment specialist to operate the scheme. Failure to comply can result in prosecution, unlimited fines, and custodial sentences in the event of an outbreak.
    </Warning>

    ### Monthly Checks

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Check Sump Water Level and Make-Up Water Supply">
        Inspect the sump water level against the marked operating level or float valve setting. A low sump level indicates a make-up water supply fault (failed float valve, closed isolating valve, or low mains pressure) or excessive bleed-off. A high sump level may indicate a failed float valve allowing continuous fill. Correct the water level to within the design operating range. Log the reading.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Clean Sump Screen and Strainer">
        Remove and clean the sump intake screen and circulation pump strainer basket. Rinse clean with fresh water and inspect the mesh for holes or damage — a damaged screen allows debris to reach the spray nozzles and pump impeller. Reinstall securely. Note any accumulation of scale, algae, or debris in the sump and arrange for cleaning if biological or scale growth is apparent.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Water Treatment Parameters">
        Test the sump water using the test kit provided by your water treatment specialist. Record and compare to programme targets:
        - **Biocide residual**: confirm effective concentration per the treatment scheme (commonly oxidising biocides such as chlorine or bromine, or non-oxidising biocides on a scheduled dosing programme)
        - **Scale inhibitor and corrosion inhibitor**: confirm dosing pump operation and residual levels
        - **Bleed-off (conductivity)**: verify the bleed-off rate is maintaining conductivity within target limits to prevent excessive scale concentration
        - **pH**: typically 7.5–8.5 for Legionella control and material compatibility

        Submit results to your water treatment specialist and take corrective action on any parameter outside target range without delay.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Spray Nozzles for Blockage">
        With the water pump running, observe all spray nozzles across the coil section. Each nozzle should produce a uniform, even spray pattern that wets the entire coil surface above it. A blocked or partially blocked nozzle produces a reduced or distorted spray pattern, leaving dry areas on the coil that dramatically reduce heat transfer. Remove and clean any blocked nozzle with clean water and a soft brush or compressed air. Do not use sharp instruments that might enlarge the nozzle orifice.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Fan and Pump Amp Draws">
        Using a clamp meter, measure and record the amp draw of the circulation pump motor and each condenser fan motor. Compare to nameplate FLA values. Elevated pump amp draw may indicate a blocked impeller or worn bearings. Elevated fan amp draw may indicate motor winding degradation, bearing wear, or incorrect capacitor. Record all values for trend monitoring.
      </Step>
    </Steps>

    ### Annual Service

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Full Drain, Sump Clean, and Descale">
        At least once per year (or more frequently if the water treatment specialist advises), carry out a full shut-down, drain, and clean of the evaporative condenser:
        1. Isolate the unit, drain the sump fully, and remove all standing water.
        2. Remove accumulated scale, sludge, and biological deposits from the sump, spray headers, and all internal surfaces using hot water, stiff brushes, and where necessary an approved descaling agent (dilute acid or chelating agent as recommended by the water treatment specialist).
        3. Rinse all surfaces thoroughly with clean fresh water.
        4. Refill the sump with fresh water and dose immediately with start-up biocide and inhibitor per the treatment programme before returning to operation.
        
        Log the date and details of the clean in the Legionella control logbook.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Fill Media and Packing">
        Inspect the evaporative fill media (also called packing or wet deck fill) — this is the structured plastic or ceramic material over which sump water flows to maximise evaporative surface area. Look for physical damage (cracking, crushing, or deformation from impact or UV degradation), heavy scale deposits, and biological growth (pink or black biofilm). Heavily fouled or damaged fill media significantly reduces performance. Clean recoverable fouling with a biocide solution and low-pressure rinse; replace fill sections that are physically damaged or irrecoverable.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check and Replace Drift Eliminators if Damaged">
        Drift eliminators are installed above the spray zone to capture and return water droplets that would otherwise be carried out of the unit by the airstream. Intact drift eliminators are critical for water conservation and, more importantly, for **Legionella risk control** — drift carrying Legionella-contaminated water droplets is the primary route of infection for people near cooling towers and evaporative condensers. Inspect the drift eliminator pack for warping, cracking, missing sections, or biological fouling. Replace any section showing physical damage or deterioration. Record the inspection result in the Legionella control logbook.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Legionella Risk Assessment Review">
        At least annually, commission a formal review of the Legionella risk assessment for the evaporative condenser by a competent person in accordance with ACOP L8 and HSG274 Part 1. The review should consider: any changes to the system (new pipework, changed operating regime, new spray devices), the water treatment scheme performance, any microbiological results from the year, and any new guidance or regulatory changes. Update the written risk assessment record to reflect any changes. File the updated assessment in the site Legionella control log.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Water Sample for Legionella Analysis">
        Collect a water sample from the evaporative condenser sump in accordance with the sampling protocol defined in the Legionella risk assessment (typically using a sterile sample container, without prior biocide dosing, and submitted to an accredited laboratory within the required holding time). The laboratory result should be compared against the action levels defined in HSG274:
        - **< 100 cfu/L**: satisfactory; maintain scheme
        - **100–999 cfu/L**: review treatment; resample within 2 weeks
        - **≥ 1,000 cfu/L**: immediate action required — review and intensify treatment; notify relevant parties per the risk assessment action plan

        Record all sample results in the Legionella control logbook. Do not file and forget — adverse results require a documented response.
      </Step>
    </Steps>
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Troubleshooting: High Condensing Pressure

High condensing pressure is the most common and consequential symptom of a poorly maintained condenser. Use the table below as a first-stage diagnostic guide.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Air-Cooled: Dirty Coil or Blocked Airflow" icon="wind">
    **Symptom**: Condensing temperature more than 15°C above ambient.
    
    **Check**: Coil face for fouling; confirm all fans running at correct rotation; check for obstructions blocking air inlet or discharge.
    
    **Action**: Clean coil; restore fan operation; remove obstructions.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Air-Cooled: Fan Motor or Capacitor Failure" icon="bolt">
    **Symptom**: One or more fans not running; amp draw zero or excessive on the failed motor.
    
    **Check**: Capacitor capacitance; motor winding resistance; motor overload reset.
    
    **Action**: Replace capacitor or motor as required; reset overload.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Water-Cooled: Scale Fouling on Tubes" icon="droplets">
    **Symptom**: Condensing temperature–to–water outlet temperature differential increasing over time; rising water outlet temperature.
    
    **Check**: Tube cleanliness (via waterside inspection); water treatment records.
    
    **Action**: Tube cleaning; review water treatment programme.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Water-Cooled: Reduced Water Flow" icon="gauge">
    **Symptom**: High condenser water ΔT (>7–8°C); low flow rate on flow meter.
    
    **Check**: Strainer basket for blockage; pump running and delivering correct duty; WRV position.
    
    **Action**: Clean strainer; restore pump; service or replace WRV.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Evaporative: Dry Coil (Spray Failure)" icon="spray-can">
    **Symptom**: High condensing pressure with adequate fan operation; dry patches visible on coil.
    
    **Check**: Sump level; pump operation; spray nozzle condition.
    
    **Action**: Restore sump level; unblock or replace nozzles; service circulation pump.
  </Card>
  <Card title="All Types: Refrigerant Overcharge" icon="thermometer">
    **Symptom**: High discharge pressure with clean condenser and correct airflow or water flow; high subcooling.
    
    **Check**: Subcooling at condenser outlet (> 8–10°C indicates overcharge).
    
    **Action**: Recover excess refrigerant to achieve target subcooling of 3–6°C; update F-gas log.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>
