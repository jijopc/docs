Provision refrigeration plant — encompassing condensing units, evaporators, controls, pipework, and refrigerant systems — is among the most critical mechanical infrastructure in food service and cold storage facilities. Consistent preventive maintenance protects product integrity, ensures regulatory compliance, and extends equipment life. The procedures below apply to systems using common refrigerants including R404A, R448A, and R452A, and should be performed by a competent, F-gas certificated refrigeration engineer or supervised technician.

<Warning>
  Under EU and UK F-Gas Regulations, systems with a refrigerant charge equivalent to **5 tonnes CO₂ or more** must be leak-checked at mandatory intervals and all refrigerant additions must be logged in a compliant F-gas equipment register. Failure to maintain records is a legal offence and can result in significant fines. Ensure your F-gas logbook is kept up to date at every service visit.
</Warning>

<Note>
  Every time refrigerant is added to a system — even a small top-up — you must record the quantity added, the refrigerant type, the date, the engineer's F-gas certificate number, and the reason for addition in the equipment's F-gas log. This is a legal requirement, not optional best practice.
</Note>

<Info>
  R404A has a Global Warming Potential (GWP) of 3,922 and is subject to phase-down restrictions under F-Gas regulations. Reclaimed or recycled R404A may still be used for servicing existing equipment, but virgin R404A is no longer available for system top-ups in the UK and EU. Where possible, consider planning a refrigerant retrofit to a lower-GWP alternative such as R448A (GWP 1,387) or R452A (GWP 2,140) at your next major service.
</Info>

## Maintenance Schedule

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Daily">
    Carry out daily checks at the start and end of each operational period — or at a minimum twice per day. These checks take approximately 15–20 minutes and catch developing faults before they cause product loss or compressor failure.

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Record Evaporator and Condenser Temperatures">
        Read and log the evaporator coil temperature and the condenser air-off or water-out temperature at each check. Compare readings against the previous entries. A rising evaporator temperature trend often indicates a refrigerant loss, dirty coil, or defrost fault. Record values in the site logbook with time and date.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Compressor Suction and Discharge Pressures">
        Using the system's gauge set or installed pressure gauges, read suction pressure (low side) and discharge pressure (high side). Convert to saturation temperatures using a pressure–temperature chart for the refrigerant in use (R404A, R448A, or R452A). Suction saturation temperature should correspond to evaporator design conditions; abnormally low suction pressure indicates refrigerant loss or a restricted metering device. High discharge pressure indicates a condenser or heat rejection problem.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Listen for Unusual Compressor Noise">
        Stand near the compressor with the plant running and listen for any knocking, rattling, or hissing sounds. Knocking may indicate liquid slugging or worn bearings. Hissing at valves or fittings may indicate a refrigerant or oil leak. Report any unusual noise immediately and do not defer investigation.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Confirm Defrost Operation">
        Verify that defrosts are initiating and terminating correctly. On electronic controllers, check the defrost history log if available. Confirm that the evaporator coil returns to normal operating temperature within the expected time after defrost completion. Incomplete defrosts cause ice accumulation that progressively reduces airflow and cooling capacity.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Case or Room Temperature Against Setpoint">
        Read the display or room temperature from each controller and verify it is within the acceptable band of the setpoint. A persistent deviation of more than ±1–2°C from setpoint warrants investigation. Check that room or case doors are not being held open.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Door Seals on Cold Rooms">
        Open and close each cold room door and inspect the full perimeter of the door gasket. Look for tears, compression damage, or ice formation around the seal. Ice building up on the door frame or gasket is a strong indicator that the seal is not closing properly, allowing warm moist air ingress. Replace damaged gaskets promptly.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Liquid Line Sight Glass">
        Observe the sight glass in the liquid line. A clear, bubble-free flow indicates adequate refrigerant charge. Bubbles in the sight glass indicate either low refrigerant charge or excessive subcooling loss — investigate further. Also check the moisture indicator element: green indicates dry, yellow or gold indicates moisture in the system. A wet indication requires immediate investigation and filter drier replacement.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Condensate Drain Trays">
        Check drain trays under each evaporator coil. Trays should be clear of standing water, ice, algae, or debris. A blocked drain will result in water overflow, product contamination, or slip hazards. If drain lines are electrically trace-heated, confirm the trace heating is energised.
      </Step>
    </Steps>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Monthly">
    Monthly tasks require a competent refrigeration engineer and should be scheduled during a low-traffic operational window. Allow 1–3 hours depending on plant size and number of circuits.

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Clean Condenser Coil (Air-Cooled)">
        Isolate the condenser fan(s) and blow out the coil fins with dry compressed air, working from the clean side through to the dirty side to push debris clear of the coil face. For heavier soiling, use an approved non-acidic foaming coil cleaner applied with a hand pump sprayer. Allow the foam to dwell as directed by the manufacturer, then rinse with low-pressure water. Do not use high-pressure water jets directly on fins as this bends the fin array and reduces airflow. Inspect the coil surface after cleaning and note its condition in the service record.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check and Clean Evaporator Coil">
        With the system defrosted, inspect the evaporator coil face. If ice is present after a completed defrost cycle, the defrost is not fully effective — increase defrost duration or frequency. Clean the coil surface using approved evaporator coil cleaner if fouling is present. Inspect fin condition and note any physical damage. Restricted airflow through the evaporator directly reduces system capacity and increases runtime.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Refrigerant Pipe Insulation">
        Walk all accessible suction and liquid line pipework. Check insulation for gaps at joints, compression damage, moisture absorption (soft, wet insulation), or missing sections. Damaged suction line insulation causes condensation and heat gain, raising suction temperature and reducing efficiency. Repair all defects using closed-cell insulation and self-amalgamating tape or purpose-made insulation fittings.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Tighten Electrical Connections and Inspect Contactors">
        With the power isolated and locked off in accordance with site LOTO procedures, open the electrical panel and control enclosures. Tighten all terminal screws and busbar connections. Inspect contactors for signs of burning, pitting, or contact erosion — discoloured contacts or arc marks indicate the contactor is approaching end of life and should be replaced. Check thermal overload relay settings against motor full-load amp (FLA) ratings on the nameplate.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Compressor Oil Level and Condition">
        Observe the compressor crankcase sight glass (where fitted). Oil level should be visible in the middle of the sight glass during operation. Dark or foamy oil may indicate refrigerant migration into the oil or oil degradation — this warrants further investigation and possibly an oil sample for analysis. Low oil level must be investigated; do not simply top up without identifying the cause.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Verify All Safety Controls">
        Test and verify the operation of the following safety devices:
        - **High-pressure (HP) switch**: manually raise condensing pressure or use a dedicated test to confirm the HP switch trips at its rated set-point and requires manual reset.
        - **Low-pressure (LP) switch**: confirm it cuts out the compressor at the set low-pressure limit.
        - **Oil pressure differential switch** (reciprocating compressors): confirm it trips within the set time-delay if oil pressure differential is lost.
        - **High-temperature alarm**: confirm the room or case temperature alarm activates at the set threshold.

        Record the tested trip points and confirm against the design set-points.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Control Board Setpoints and Alarm Settings">
        Review all setpoints on electronic controllers: cut-in and cut-out temperatures, defrost initiation time(s), defrost duration, defrost termination temperature, and alarm thresholds. Confirm settings have not drifted or been altered without authorisation. Record current setpoints in the service record.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Verify Defrost Controller Settings">
        Check the defrost timeclock or electronic defrost controller. Confirm the number of defrosts per 24 hours, the defrost start times, and the termination temperature or time-override setting are appropriate for the current season and product loading. Adjust defrost frequency in summer if ice accumulation is increasing. Verify the hot gas defrost solenoid or electric defrost heater contactor is operating correctly.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Cold Room Doors, Hinges, and Door Closers">
        Open each cold room door fully and inspect the hinge pintles for wear. Check that the door closer (hydraulic or spring-loaded) returns the door to the fully closed and sealed position within a reasonable time. A door that remains ajar increases refrigeration load significantly. Adjust closer tension as required. Check anti-condensation door frame heaters are functioning where fitted.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check and Clean Condensate Drain Lines">
        Flush each condensate drain line with hot water to clear algae, food debris, and scale. Where electric trace heating is fitted to drain lines, verify it is energised and functioning. Apply a suitable drain biocide tablet or solution to the drain tray to inhibit bacterial and algal growth. Check the drain exits clear of the building and is not causing a slip hazard externally.
      </Step>
    </Steps>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Annual">
    Annual maintenance is the primary compliance service visit. It must be carried out by an F-gas certificated refrigeration engineer. Allow a full day per plant room or system grouping. Complete the F-gas equipment log at this visit.

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Full Refrigerant Leak Test">
        Conduct a comprehensive refrigerant leak test of the entire system using a calibrated electronic refrigerant sniffer appropriate for the refrigerant in use. Test all brazed and flared joints, Schrader valve cores, solenoid valve bonnets, expansion valve connections, sight glass fittings, compressor shaft seals (open-drive only), and all other potential leak points. If a leak is found, repair before recharging and retest. Record the result (leak-free or leak found and repaired) in the F-gas log.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Update F-Gas Equipment Log">
        Complete all required entries in the F-gas equipment log including:
        - Date of service
        - Refrigerant type and system charge quantity
        - Any refrigerant added or recovered (quantity in kg)
        - Leak test result
        - Engineer's name and F-gas certificate number
        - Operator signature

        For systems with ≥5 tonnes CO₂ equivalent charge, this documentation is a statutory requirement. Retain records for a minimum of five years.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Oil Change or Oil Analysis">
        On reciprocating and screw compressors, drain and replace the compressor oil at the interval specified by the compressor manufacturer (typically every 12–24 months or after a system contamination event). Where an oil analysis programme is in place, send a sample to the laboratory and review the report for metal particles, acidity, moisture, or refrigerant dilution before deciding on a change. Always use the correct oil specification for the refrigerant and compressor type.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Replace Filter Drier">
        Replace the liquid line filter drier cartridge or sealed unit. A saturated filter drier will allow moisture into the system, causing ice blockages at the expansion valve and acid formation in the oil. Check the moisture indicator in the sight glass before and after replacement to confirm the new drier is effective. Record the replacement in the service record.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Check Refrigerant Charge: Superheat and Subcooling">
        With the system running at stable conditions:
        - Measure **suction superheat** at the evaporator outlet: attach a temperature clamp to the suction line at the evaporator outlet, read suction pressure, and convert to saturation temperature. Subtract to get superheat. Target range: **5–10°C superheat**. Low superheat risks liquid return to the compressor; high superheat indicates undercharge or a restricted expansion valve.
        - Measure **subcooling** at the condenser outlet: attach a temperature clamp to the liquid line leaving the condenser, read discharge/liquid line pressure, convert to saturation temperature, and subtract the actual liquid temperature. Target range: **3–6°C subcooling**. Low subcooling indicates undercharge or excessive liquid line heat gain.

        Adjust the refrigerant charge or expansion valve setting as required.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Full Electrical Inspection">
        Carry out a full inspection of the electrical panel, control panel, and all associated wiring:
        - Check cable condition, terminations, and cable management for heat damage, rodent damage, or chafing.
        - Carry out a megger (insulation resistance) test on compressor and fan motor windings — record values and compare to previous year to identify degradation trends.
        - Verify that all circuit protection (fuses, MCBs, motor starters) is correctly rated and in good condition.
        - Check earth continuity on all exposed metalwork.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Test and Calibrate Temperature Controllers and Probes">
        Using a calibrated reference thermometer, verify the accuracy of all temperature probes and controllers. Place the reference probe adjacent to the system probe and compare readings at a stable temperature. A deviation of more than ±0.5°C should trigger probe replacement or recalibration. Update calibration records. Check all thermostat differential settings and confirm they match the design specification.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Test All Alarms">
        Functionally test each alarm:
        - **High-temperature alarm**: raise the room or case temperature (or simulate via controller) to confirm the alarm activates at the set threshold, and that any remote monitoring or BMS signal is transmitted correctly.
        - **Door-open alarm**: hold the door open beyond the set time delay and confirm the alarm activates.
        - **Power-failure alarm**: simulate a power interruption (if safe to do so) and confirm the alarm is transmitted to the remote monitoring system or building management system.

        Record the test results and any corrective actions.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Clean and Treat Drain Pans with Biocide">
        Thoroughly clean all condensate and defrost drain pans with hot water and an approved biocide solution. Remove all organic matter, biofilm, and scale. Apply a residual biocide treatment to inhibit regrowth. Inspect drain pans for corrosion or physical damage — replace if the pan integrity is compromised.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Inspect Cold Room Structure">
        Carry out a visual inspection of the cold room structure:
        - **Panels**: check insulated panel faces and joints for damage, delamination, or moisture ingress (soft panels indicate moisture within the insulation core).
        - **Floor**: check for cracking, drainage falls, and any frost heave under the floor slab (if no sub-floor heating is fitted, this is a risk in freezer rooms).
        - **Ceiling**: inspect for condensation, structural deflection, or ceiling panel damage.
        - **Lighting**: check all cold room light fittings are suitable for the temperature zone, free of moisture ingress, and that lamp output is adequate.

        Record findings and raise any structural defects for remediation.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Update Service Records and F-Gas Compliance Documentation">
        Complete the full service record, including all test results, measurements, parts replaced, and engineer sign-off. File the updated F-gas log with the site records. Provide the site contact with a written report of the service, any advisory items, and any outstanding remedial works. Confirm the date of the next mandatory service visit.
      </Step>
    </Steps>
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Frequently Asked Questions

<Accordion title="How often must F-gas leak checks be carried out?">
  The mandatory leak check frequency depends on the system's CO₂ equivalent charge:
  - **5–50 tonnes CO₂e**: check every **12 months**
  - **50–500 tonnes CO₂e**: check every **6 months**
  - **≥500 tonnes CO₂e**: check every **3 months**

  Systems with an approved continuous electronic leak detection system have the above intervals doubled. All checks and their results must be recorded in the F-gas equipment log.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What should I do if refrigerant bubbles appear in the sight glass?">
  Bubbles in the liquid line sight glass almost always indicate a problem. First, check the system's suction and discharge pressures to assess whether the system is undercharged or whether a restriction is causing localised flash gas. Do not add refrigerant without first carrying out a full leak test and confirming the root cause. If a leak is found, repair it before recharging. Record any refrigerant addition in the F-gas log with the quantity, refrigerant type, and engineer certification number.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What superheat and subcooling values should I target?">
  For most direct-expansion (DX) provision refrigeration systems:
  - **Evaporator suction superheat**: 5–10°C (measured at the evaporator outlet)
  - **Condenser subcooling**: 3–6°C (measured at the condenser liquid outlet)

  These values vary by system design, refrigerant, and load conditions. Always refer to the system manufacturer's design data where available, and use actual operating pressures and temperatures rather than rule-of-thumb values alone.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What is the impact of a failed door seal on refrigeration performance?">
  A damaged or poorly sealing cold room door gasket allows continuous infiltration of warm, humid ambient air. This dramatically increases the refrigeration load (latent and sensible heat), causes excessive frost and ice accumulation on the evaporator and door frame, and raises energy consumption significantly. In freezer rooms, sustained door seal failure can cause ice build-up under the floor slab. Replace damaged gaskets at the earliest opportunity — it is one of the highest-return maintenance actions available.
</Accordion>
