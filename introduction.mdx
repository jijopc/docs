Preventive maintenance (PM) is a proactive, scheduled approach to servicing HVAC equipment before failures occur. Rather than waiting for a breakdown to force your hand, a structured PM program keeps chillers, air handling units, split systems, packaged units, and refrigeration plants running at peak efficiency year-round. When you commit to regular inspection, cleaning, lubrication, and calibration cycles, you dramatically reduce unplanned downtime, lower energy consumption, and extend the useful life of capital-intensive mechanical and refrigeration equipment.

## Why Preventive Maintenance Matters

A well-executed PM program delivers measurable returns across four critical areas:

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Extended Equipment Life" icon="clock">
    Routine lubrication, belt tensioning, and coil cleaning prevent the accelerated wear that shortens equipment lifespan. Most commercial HVAC systems designed for 15–25 years of service fall short of that target without consistent maintenance.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Energy Efficiency" icon="bolt">
    Dirty condenser coils, low refrigerant charge, clogged filters, and misaligned belts can increase energy consumption by 10–40%. Scheduled PM restores system efficiency and reduces operating costs directly.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Reduced Downtime" icon="wrench">
    Identifying worn bearings, refrigerant leaks, and fouled heat exchangers during scheduled visits prevents catastrophic failures that strand occupants and trigger expensive emergency service calls.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Warranty Compliance" icon="shield-check">
    Most OEM warranties require documented evidence of scheduled maintenance. Skipping PM intervals can void coverage on compressors, heat exchangers, and controls — often the most expensive components to replace.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Scope of This Guide

This guide covers the full range of commercial and industrial HVAC and refrigeration equipment commonly found in commercial buildings, process facilities, and data centers:

- **Chiller Systems** — centrifugal, screw, scroll, and absorption chillers
- **Air Handling Units (AHUs)** — supply, return, and makeup air units
- **DX Split Air Conditioners** — single-split, multi-split, and VRF/VRV systems
- **Water-Cooled Packaged AC Units** — self-contained water-cooled package systems
- **Air-Cooled Packaged AC Units** — rooftop units (RTUs) and air-cooled self-contained systems
- **Provision Refrigeration Plants** — industrial and commercial refrigeration racks and systems
- **Condensers** — evaporative, air-cooled, and water-cooled condensers
- **Compressors** — reciprocating, rotary, screw, scroll, and centrifugal types

## Who Should Perform Maintenance

All maintenance tasks described in this guide must be carried out by **qualified HVAC technicians** or **licensed refrigeration engineers** with the appropriate certifications and training for the work being performed. Specifically:

- Refrigerant handling, recovery, and charging require **EPA Section 608 certification** (or the applicable national equivalent)
- Electrical work must be performed by personnel qualified to the voltage levels involved, with appropriate arc flash training
- High-pressure boiler or chilled water system work may require additional licensure depending on your jurisdiction
- Brazing and welding operations require a valid **hot work permit** and appropriate competency credentials

Unqualified personnel must not attempt refrigerant recovery, electrical isolation of high-voltage equipment, or any repair procedure on pressurized systems.

## Keeping Service Records

Every maintenance visit — whether routine or corrective — must be documented in a service log or your computerized maintenance management system (CMMS). Records should capture the date, technician name and certification number, equipment ID, tasks performed, parts replaced, refrigerant quantities added or recovered, and any observations about equipment condition. Thorough records support warranty claims, regulatory compliance, energy audits, and capital planning decisions.

<Note>
  Always consult the Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) installation and maintenance manual for your specific equipment model before performing any procedure. OEM specifications — including refrigerant type and charge weight, torque values, oil specifications, and filter part numbers — take precedence over the general guidance in this guide.
</Note>
