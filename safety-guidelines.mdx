Safety is the non-negotiable first priority on every HVAC maintenance job. HVAC systems expose technicians to a wide range of hazards — high-voltage electrical equipment, pressurized refrigerant circuits, rotating machinery, hazardous chemicals, and work-at-height environments — often in the same visit. Before you touch any equipment, you must understand the hazards present, have the right personal protective equipment for the job, and follow the correct isolation and permit procedures without exception. No maintenance task is urgent enough to bypass a safety procedure. If a safe working condition cannot be established, stop work and escalate.

<Warning>
  **Never begin work on any HVAC system without first completing the appropriate isolation and lockout/tagout procedure.** Failure to isolate electrical, mechanical, and pressurized energy sources before commencing work is the leading cause of serious injury and fatality in HVAC maintenance. There are no exceptions to this rule.
</Warning>

## Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) Procedure

Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) is a formal energy-isolation procedure that prevents equipment from being accidentally energized while you are working on it. Follow these steps in sequence every time, without deviation.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Identify all energy sources">
    Before touching the equipment, identify **every** energy source that must be isolated. HVAC systems may have multiple energy inputs that are not all obvious: main electrical supply, control circuit supply (often a separate transformer), gas supply, steam or hot water supply, chilled water supply, and stored mechanical energy (springs, suspended components). Consult the equipment wiring diagram and the system P&ID to confirm you have identified all sources.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Notify affected personnel">
    Inform all personnel in the affected area — including building operators, other trades, and occupants if applicable — that the equipment is being taken out of service. Record the notification in your work order. If the equipment is monitored by a BMS or building operations center, notify the operator and confirm they have acknowledged the planned outage.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Shut down the equipment using the normal operating procedure">
    Use the normal shutdown sequence for the equipment — through the controller, BMS, or local off switch. Do not use isolation as the primary shutdown method. Confirm the equipment has reached a safe stopped state before proceeding. Allow rotating components (fans, compressors) to come to a complete stop.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Isolate all identified energy sources">
    Operate every isolator, disconnect switch, valve, and breaker identified in Step 1 to the de-energized or de-pressurized position. This includes:
    - **Electrical**: Open and lock the main disconnect switch or breaker. Open and lock any separate control circuit isolators.
    - **Gas**: Close and lock the manual gas shutoff valve upstream of the equipment.
    - **Chilled/hot water**: Close and lock the supply and return isolation valves.
    - **Refrigerant**: Close service valves at the compressor suction and discharge where required by the procedure.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply your personal lock and tag to every isolation point">
    Place **your own padlock** on every energy isolation point — each technician working on the equipment must apply their own lock. Attach a durable, legible lockout tag showing your name, contact number, date, and the reason for lockout. Do not rely on another person's lock as your only protection. If there are more isolation points than lock hasps allow, use a lockout hasp or lockout station to accommodate multiple locks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Release or restrain residual and stored energy">
    Isolation alone does not remove all hazards. Before working, address residual stored energy:
    - **Electrical capacitors**: Discharge VFD bus capacitors using the manufacturer-specified bleed procedure and verify with a voltmeter (see [Electrical Safety](#electrical-safety)).
    - **Pressurized refrigerant circuits**: If opening the refrigerant circuit, recover refrigerant to an approved recovery cylinder — never vent to atmosphere.
    - **Pressurized water systems**: Open drain valves to relieve pressure before breaking any pipe joint.
    - **Mechanical springs**: Block or restrain any spring-loaded component (dampers, valves) in the safe position before removing fasteners.
    - **Gravity**: Support any suspended component (fan assemblies, heat exchanger shells) with approved mechanical supports before removing fasteners.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Verify zero energy state before starting work">
    Attempt to start the equipment using the normal controls to confirm it cannot be energized. Use a **calibrated, CAT III or IV voltage tester** to verify zero voltage at the load side of every electrical isolator. Check refrigerant pressure gauges to confirm system pressure is at the expected state. Only after you have verified zero energy on all sources should you begin work.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Restore energy only after work is complete and the area is clear">
    When work is complete, remove all tools and parts from inside the equipment. Confirm all guards and access panels are reinstalled. Notify all affected personnel that energy is being restored. Each technician removes only their own lock. Restore energy in the reverse order of isolation, following the normal startup sequence.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Select PPE based on the specific hazards of each task. Never substitute a lower-rated item because the correct PPE is inconvenient or unavailable — stop work and obtain the correct equipment.

<Accordion title="Electrical Work PPE">
  | PPE Item | Minimum Requirement | When Required |
  |---|---|---|
  | Safety glasses | ANSI Z87.1-rated, side shields | All electrical panel work |
  | Arc flash face shield | Arc flash rated to the incident energy level of the panel (minimum 4 cal/cm² for panels up to 240 V) | Opening energized panels, racking breakers |
  | Arc flash suit / jacket | Rated to the arc flash incident energy calculated by an arc flash study | All energized work above 50 V |
  | Insulated gloves | ASTM F496 rubber insulating gloves rated to the working voltage, with leather protectors | All energized electrical work |
  | Insulated tools | 1,000 V rated (IEC 60900) | Use inside electrical panels |
  | Safety footwear | EH-rated (electrical hazard) boots | All electrical work |
  | Hard hat | Class E (electrical) | Work near overhead electrical equipment |
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Refrigerant Handling PPE">
  | PPE Item | Minimum Requirement | When Required |
  |---|---|---|
  | Safety glasses | ANSI Z87.1 indirect-vent goggles (not open-vent safety glasses) | Any refrigerant circuit opening |
  | Refrigerant-rated gloves | Cryogenic or chemical-resistant gloves rated for refrigerant contact | Connecting recovery hoses, opening service valves |
  | Face shield | Chemical splash-rated | Risk of liquid refrigerant spray |
  | Respiratory protection | Self-Contained Breathing Apparatus (SCBA) or supplied-air respirator for confined spaces with refrigerant risk; half-face respirator with organic vapor cartridge for low-level exposure | Refrigerant leak investigation in enclosed spaces |
  | Safety footwear | Chemical-resistant boots | Large refrigerant handling operations |

## Refrigerant Handling Safety

<Warning>
  **Venting refrigerant to the atmosphere is illegal under EPA Section 608 regulations (40 CFR Part 82) and equivalent regulations in most jurisdictions worldwide.** Intentional venting of any refrigerant — including refrigerants previously exempt from this rule — can result in civil penalties of up to $44,539 per day per violation. Always recover refrigerant to an approved recovery cylinder before opening any part of a refrigerant circuit.
</Warning>

Refrigerant handling must be performed only by technicians holding a current **EPA Section 608 certification** (Type I, II, III, or Universal as appropriate for the equipment) or the equivalent national certification in your jurisdiction.

Follow these refrigerant safety practices on every job:

- **Always use a manifold gauge set with service hoses rated for the refrigerant and pressure involved.** Do not use R-22 hoses on HFO or HFO/HFC blend systems.
- **Recover before opening.** Connect an approved refrigerant recovery machine and a properly labeled, DOT-approved recovery cylinder before breaking any refrigerant connection. Weigh the cylinder before and after recovery and document the quantity recovered.
- **Check the cylinder fill level.** Never fill a recovery cylinder beyond 80% of its rated gross weight. Overfilled cylinders are a rupture hazard.
- **Identify the refrigerant type before recovery.** Never mix refrigerants in a recovery cylinder. Use a refrigerant identifier if the refrigerant type is uncertain.
- **Use a refrigerant leak detector** before opening equipment. If refrigerant vapor concentration in a confined space is detectable, ventilate the space thoroughly and wear appropriate respiratory protection before entering.
- **Keep recovery cylinders cool.** Do not expose cylinders to direct sunlight or temperatures above 125°F (52°C).
- **Purge and pressure-test after opening.** After completing repairs, pressure-test with dry nitrogen (never oxygen) to the OEM-specified test pressure before recharging. Perform an electronic leak test before charging.

## Electrical Safety

HVAC equipment operates at voltages from 24 VAC (control circuits) to 480 VAC or higher (large chiller compressors and VFDs). Treat every electrical circuit as energized until you have personally verified it is de-energized.

- **Verify with a calibrated voltage tester.** Use a CAT III or CAT IV rated digital multimeter or non-contact voltage tester. Test before touching, and test again after touching — a single verification is not sufficient.
- **Discharge VFD bus capacitors.** Variable frequency drives store potentially lethal DC voltage on DC bus capacitors after power is removed. Follow the VFD manufacturer's specific discharge procedure — typically waiting a minimum of 5 minutes after power removal and verifying DC bus voltage below 50 VDC with a voltmeter before touching internal components.
- **Check for back-feed.** Equipment connected to multiple power sources (e.g., dual-supply, generator back-feed, UPS) can remain energized even after the primary disconnect is opened. Identify and isolate all supplies.
- **Never work alone on energized equipment.** A second person must be present and aware of the hazard whenever energized work above 50 V is performed. The second person must know how to shut off power and perform CPR.
- **Use insulated, rated tools.** Use 1,000 V-rated insulated screwdrivers, pliers, and wrenches inside electrical panels.
- **Maintain safe approach distances.** Follow NFPA 70E arc flash approach boundaries calculated by your site's arc flash study. Do not enter the restricted approach boundary without proper PPE and authorization.

## Working at Height

Rooftop units, cooling towers, and elevated plant rooms require work at height. Falls are among the most common causes of fatal injury in the building services industry.

- **Inspect ladders before every use.** Check for cracked rails, missing rungs, and damaged feet. Never use a damaged ladder. Maintain three points of contact when climbing. Set extension ladders at a 75-degree angle (4:1 ratio — 1 foot out for every 4 feet of height).
- **Use the correct access equipment for the task.** For sustained work at height on a rooftop, a fall arrest system is safer than a ladder. For elevated indoor plant, use an approved mobile elevated work platform (MEWP) where practicable.
- **Establish a fall arrest anchor before stepping onto an unguarded surface.** Connect your harness lanyard to a certified anchor before you are at risk of falling.
- **Tether all tools at height.** Use tool lanyards or tool pouches to prevent dropped tools from injuring personnel or equipment below.
- **Establish an exclusion zone below.** Cordon off the area below elevated work to prevent personnel from entering the drop zone.
- **Be aware of roof loading limits and fragile surfaces.** Do not walk on fragile roof panels, skylights, or unsupported sheet metal. Walk on structural members or use roof boards to spread load.

## Hot Work Permits

Brazing, welding, and soldering — common in refrigerant pipe repair — are classified as hot work and require a **hot work permit** from the site's responsible person before commencing.

- Obtain a signed hot work permit before lighting any torch.
- Clear the work area of flammable materials and refrigerant containers to a minimum of 10 meters (33 feet).
- Have a serviceable dry powder or CO₂ fire extinguisher within arm's reach at all times during hot work.
- Assign a dedicated **fire watch** — a second person whose sole responsibility is monitoring for ignition during and for 30 minutes after hot work.
- Purge the section of pipe with dry nitrogen before and during brazing to prevent internal oxidation and to ensure no refrigerant vapour is present. Refrigerant vapour in contact with an open flame produces toxic phosgene gas.
- After hot work is complete, inspect the work area thoroughly for smoldering materials before the fire watch period ends.

## Emergency Procedures

### Refrigerant Leak Response

If you detect or suspect a significant refrigerant release:

1. **Do not enter the affected area without appropriate respiratory protection** (SCBA or supplied-air in a confined space; organic vapor respirator for open areas with detectable vapor).
2. Evacuate non-essential personnel from the affected area immediately.
3. Ventilate the space — open doors and activate exhaust fans if safe to do so without entering the area.
4. Shut off ignition sources in the affected area if it is safe to do so remotely. Some refrigerants, including HFO-1234yf and R-32, have low flammability ratings and present an ignition risk.
5. Contact the building facilities manager, safety officer, and — for large releases — local emergency services (fire department with HazMat capability).
6. Do not attempt to repair the leak until the area has been ventilated and refrigerant vapor concentration verified safe with a calibrated detector.
7. Document the incident, quantity of refrigerant released (estimated), and actions taken. Report to the EPA or relevant authority if required by regulation. Section 608 reporting thresholds apply to appliances with 50 lbs or more of refrigerant charge.

### First Aid and Emergency Contacts

<Info>
  Post the following contact information in every mechanical plant room and on every work order. Verify contact numbers are current before starting work on each site.
</Info>

| Contact | Information to Post |
|---|---|
| Site emergency number | Site-specific — confirm before each job |
| Poison Control Center (US) | 1-800-222-1222 |
| National Response Center (US refrigerant spill) | 1-800-424-8802 |
| Site facilities manager | Site-specific |
| Nearest hospital with emergency department | Site-specific |

**Refrigerant skin/eye contact:** Flush with large quantities of clean water for a minimum of 15 minutes. Seek medical attention immediately. Do not rub the affected area.

**Refrigerant inhalation:** Move the person to fresh air immediately. If breathing has stopped, administer CPR if you are trained to do so. Call emergency services. Do not re-enter the contaminated area without respiratory protection.

**Electrical shock:** Do not touch the victim while they are in contact with the energy source. De-energize the circuit at the nearest isolator before approaching. Call emergency services. If the victim is unresponsive and not breathing normally, begin CPR.
