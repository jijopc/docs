sidebarTitle: "DX Split AC"
description: "Monthly and annual PM for DX split and VRF air conditioners. Covers indoor and outdoor unit tasks, refrigerant verification, and F-gas compliance guidance."
---

Direct expansion (DX) split air conditioners transfer heat by circulating refrigerant between an indoor evaporator unit and an outdoor condensing unit, with the refrigerant itself acting as the heat transfer medium — unlike chilled water systems, which use water as an intermediary. Modern installations span four main configurations: **wall-mount** units (the most common, for individual rooms or zones), **ceiling cassettes** (recessed into suspended ceilings for 360° air distribution), **ducted fan coil units** (concealed above a ceiling void with short duct runs), and **multi-split VRF (Variable Refrigerant Flow)** systems (one large outdoor unit serving multiple indoor units with independent zone control). All configurations share the same fundamental maintenance requirements, with additional considerations for multi-split systems covered in the section below.

<Warning>
  **Never use a high-pressure jet washer or pressure cleaner on coil fins.** The aluminium fins on both indoor evaporator and outdoor condenser coils are extremely delicate. High-pressure water will bend and flatten fins, permanently restricting airflow and reducing heat transfer efficiency. Always use low-pressure water (hand-held garden hose pressure or lower), directed with the fin orientation, or use a no-rinse foaming coil cleaner for indoor coils.
</Warning>

## Maintenance Schedule

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Monthly — Indoor Unit">
    Carry out these tasks on every indoor unit in the installation each month. A complete record for each unit — including its location reference, filter condition, and any fault codes — should be entered in the service log.

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Clean the Air Filters">
        Most wall-mount and cassette indoor units use washable polypropylene mesh filters. Remove the filters according to the manufacturer's procedure (usually by lifting the front panel and unclipping the filter frames).

        To clean:
        1. Take filters outside or to a utility area — shake off loose dust first.
        2. Wash with **warm water** and a mild detergent, using your hands or a soft sponge to work out embedded dirt.
        3. Rinse thoroughly until the water runs clear.
        4. Allow filters to **air-dry completely** in shade before reinstalling — fitting a damp filter promotes mould growth on the evaporator coil.

        <Warning>
          Do not use hot water, as it can warp the filter frame. Do not scrub a wet filter with a stiff brush — this forces dirt deeper into the mesh and can damage the fibres.
        </Warning>

        <Tip>
          In high-usage periods — such as peak summer or winter operation, or spaces with above-average dust levels (gyms, workshops, reception areas with heavy footfall) — clean filters every two weeks rather than monthly. A clogged filter is the single most common cause of evaporator icing, reduced airflow, and compressor overload in DX split systems.
        </Tip>
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check Supply Air Temperature">
        Using a calibrated thermometer or the unit's building management system (BMS) integration, measure the supply air temperature at the unit outlet and compare it to the setpoint displayed on the controller. In cooling mode, a correctly operating system should deliver supply air approximately **8–12°C below return air temperature**. A supply air temperature significantly warmer than expected (with the setpoint calling for cooling) suggests a dirty evaporator coil, low refrigerant charge, or a faulty expansion valve.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Inspect the Evaporator Coil">
        With the filter removed, inspect the evaporator coil face through the open filter access. Look for:
        - **Visible dirt or dust accumulation** between the fins — a light grey coating is normal after several weeks; a thick mat of compacted dust indicates the filter has been bypassed or is not sealing correctly around the edges.
        - **Ice formation** — any frost or ice on the coil during normal operation is abnormal. Shut the unit down, investigate the root cause (blocked airflow, dirty coil, or low refrigerant), and resolve before restarting.
        - **Oil streaking** on the coil surface — oil stains indicate a refrigerant leak, as refrigerant oil travels with the refrigerant and deposits at the leak point.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Inspect the Drain Pan and Confirm Drainage">
        Locate the condensate drain pan beneath the evaporator coil (the location varies by unit type — front-accessible on most wall-mounts, accessible via the service panel on cassettes). Check for:
        - **Standing water** — none should be present; if water is present, the drain line is blocked.
        - **Debris or slime** — wipe out any accumulation with a damp cloth.

        To confirm the drain line is clear, slowly pour approximately **500 ml of clean water** into the drain pan tray and observe that it drains freely within 30 seconds. If water backs up, clear the drain line by flushing with water or using a vacuum pump at the outdoor drain outlet. Do not leave without confirming drainage — a blocked drain will cause the unit to flood the ceiling or wall below.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check for Musty or Mouldy Odour">
        Run the unit in fan-only mode briefly and stand in the air stream. A musty, earthy, or 'locker room' odour is a reliable indicator of **mould or bacterial biofilm growth on the evaporator coil and drain pan surfaces**. This is a health risk and must be treated promptly.

        Apply an HVAC-approved antimicrobial coil spray (fogging spray or aerosol) directly onto the evaporator coil and drain pan surfaces. Allow the product to dwell per the label instructions. Run the unit in fan mode afterwards to dry the coil and distribute the biocide through the drain pan. Document any treatment in the service log. Persistent odour after treatment indicates the unit requires a full chemical coil clean at the annual service.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Clean the Cabinet and Grille">
        Wipe down the unit casing and air intake/discharge grille with a clean, damp microfibre cloth. Remove accumulated dust from grille louvres and vanes. For cassette units, wipe around the ceiling panel perimeter. Do not use solvent-based cleaners on plastic housings — they cause discolouration and can make plastics brittle.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check the Display Panel for Fault Codes">
        Inspect the unit's display or LED indicator panel for any active fault codes or warning lights. Refer to the manufacturer's fault code reference sheet for the specific model. Record any codes found in the service log — do not simply reset and ignore recurring fault codes, as they indicate an underlying issue. Common codes to escalate immediately include:
        - Refrigerant high/low pressure faults
        - Communication errors between indoor and outdoor units
        - Thermistor faults (sensor failures affecting control accuracy)
        - Drain pump fault (where a drain pump is fitted)
      </Step>

      <Step title="Verify Remote Control and BMS Operation">
        Confirm the IR remote control (or wired controller) is responding correctly and the unit executes mode, setpoint, and fan speed commands as expected. For BMS-integrated units, verify in the BMS that the unit's status, mode, and setpoint are correctly reflected, and that any scheduled start/stop or temperature setback is operating as intended.
      </Step>
    </Steps>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Monthly — Outdoor Unit">
    Inspect every outdoor condensing unit on the same visit as the corresponding indoor unit(s). Outdoor unit checks typically take 10–15 minutes per unit but are critical for preventing refrigerant leaks and compressor failures from going undetected.

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Inspect Condenser Coil Fins">
        Visually inspect the condenser coil fins on all accessible sides of the outdoor unit. Fins should be clean, open, and undamaged. If the fins show dirt, leaves, seed fluff (cottonwood), or debris accumulation:
        - Remove large debris by hand.
        - Rinse the coil with a **low-pressure garden hose**, directing water from the inside of the unit outward (if access panels allow) or from outside in the direction of the fin orientation.
        - Do not use a jet washer. Do not direct water at electrical components.

        Heavily fouled condenser coils increase condensing pressure, forcing the compressor to work harder — this directly increases energy consumption and accelerates compressor wear.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check Condenser Fan Operation">
        With the unit running in cooling mode, observe the condenser fan:
        - **Rotation** — confirm the fan rotates in the correct direction (typically drawing air upward through the coil and exhausting from the top). Reverse rotation dramatically reduces condenser performance.
        - **Noise** — listen for rattling, grinding, or intermittent noise that indicates a failing fan motor bearing or an object lodged in the fan guard.
        - **Vibration** — excessive vibration suggests a bent fan blade, an unbalanced blade set, or loose mounting hardware.

        Report any abnormal noise or vibration for immediate investigation — a failed condenser fan will cause the compressor to trip on high pressure within minutes of a warm day.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Inspect Refrigerant Pipework for Leaks">
        Visually inspect the refrigerant pipes at the outdoor unit connection points (service valves and flare connections). Look specifically for:
        - **Oil staining** — a dark oily residue at pipe fittings, valve stems, or brazed joints is the most reliable visual indicator of a refrigerant leak, as compressor oil circulates with the refrigerant.
        - **Physical damage** — kinks, abrasion wear, or corrosion on the pipe insulation or copper pipe surface.
        - **Frost on pipework** — in cooling mode, mild sweating on the suction line is normal; heavy frost suggests restriction or low charge.

        If oil staining is found, log the finding and schedule a refrigerant leak detection check using an electronic refrigerant detector or UV dye inspection at the next available opportunity. Do not defer a suspected leak — refrigerant loss degrades system performance and, for F-gas refrigerants, is a regulatory compliance issue.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Verify Unit Clearances">
        Confirm that minimum clearances around the outdoor unit are maintained:
        - **Sides** — minimum **300 mm** clear of walls, fences, or other obstructions.
        - **Top (discharge)** — minimum **600 mm** clear above the fan discharge; no overhead obstruction that could cause short-circuit recirculation of hot discharge air.
        - **Front (airflow intake)** — clear of stored items, vegetation, or signage that has encroached since the last visit.

        Clearance violations cause hot air recirculation into the condenser, raising condensing pressure and reducing capacity. Arrange removal of any obstructions and document if the site configuration is non-compliant.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check Outdoor Unit Electrical Connections">
        With the unit isolated at the local isolator, open the outdoor unit terminal cover and inspect:
        - Terminal block connections for tightness — vibration can loosen screws over time.
        - Signs of overheating (discolouration, melted insulation, burnt smell).
        - Condition of the cable entry gland — must be watertight to prevent moisture tracking into the terminal block.

        Retighten any loose terminals and report any signs of overheating for immediate electrical investigation.
      </Step>
    </Steps>
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Annual">
    The annual service is a comprehensive inspection covering both indoor and outdoor components. Schedule it at the end of the cooling season (or before it begins) so the system is in peak condition for peak demand. Allow 2–4 hours per indoor/outdoor unit pair.

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Full Chemical Coil Cleaning — Indoor Evaporator">
        Perform a thorough chemical clean of the indoor evaporator coil:
        1. Protect surrounding surfaces and electrical components with plastic sheeting.
        2. Apply a no-rinse foaming coil cleaner, or a rinse-off alkaline coil cleaner appropriate for the level of fouling — follow the product data sheet for dwell time.
        3. If using a rinse-off product, flush the coil with clean water at low pressure, directing it into the drain pan.
        4. Confirm all cleaning solution has been flushed through the drain pan and drainage pipework before closing up.

        A clean evaporator coil can improve system efficiency by up to 15% compared to a moderately fouled coil.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Full Chemical Coil Cleaning — Outdoor Condenser">
        Apply an approved condenser coil cleaner (alkaline for general fouling, acid-based descaler for scale or mineral deposits common in coastal or industrial environments). Allow to dwell, then rinse thoroughly with a low-pressure hose. After cleaning, inspect the fin surface — use a fin comb to straighten any bent fins before the unit is returned to service.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Full Refrigerant System Check">
        With the system running under stable operating conditions (at least 15 minutes of runtime before taking readings), connect calibrated refrigerant gauges to the service valves and measure:

        - **Suction pressure** — compare to the saturation temperature for the refrigerant type (refer to pressure-temperature chart) to determine the **evaporating temperature**.
        - **Discharge pressure** — similarly convert to **condensing temperature**.
        - **Suction line temperature** — measure at the suction service valve with a clamp-on thermometer.

        Calculate the key performance indicators:

        | Parameter | Calculation | Target Range |
        |---|---|---|
        | **Superheat** | Suction line temp − Evaporating temp | **5–8°C** |
        | **Subcooling** | Condensing temp − Liquid line temp | **3–5°C** |

        Superheat outside the target range indicates a refrigerant charge issue or expansion device fault. Subcooling below target suggests low charge or a restriction in the liquid line. Do not add refrigerant without first confirming the cause of any deviation — adding charge to a system with a restriction will cause compressor damage.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Refrigerant Leak Detection">
        Inspect all refrigerant-containing components with an electronic refrigerant detector (confirmed to be calibrated and appropriate for the refrigerant type — HFC, HFO, or HC):
        - Flare and compression fittings at indoor and outdoor units
        - Service valve stems (Schrader cores)
        - Brazed joints on accessible pipework
        - Any previous repair points noted in the service log

        Supplement electronic detection with UV dye inspection where dye has been previously injected. Document all leak check results. Any confirmed leak must be repaired before topping up refrigerant charge, and the repair and recharge must be recorded in the F-gas equipment log (where applicable under EU/UK F-Gas Regulations or equivalent local regulations).
      </Step>

      <Step title="Clean and Treat Condensate Drain Line">
        Flush the condensate drain line with a solution of **diluted bleach (approximately 10% concentration)** or an HVAC-specific drain cleaner to dissolve biofilm and algae:
        1. Pour the solution slowly into the drain pan and allow it to flow through the line.
        2. Follow with a flush of clean water.
        3. Drop an **algaecide tablet** into the drain pan tray — these slow-release tablets inhibit biological growth in the drain pan and upper section of the drain line between services.

        For units with condensate drain pumps, test the pump operation by manually filling the float chamber and confirming the pump activates and clears the water.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Full Electrical Inspection">
        Carry out a comprehensive electrical inspection of both the indoor and outdoor unit electrical assemblies:
        - **Terminal connections** — inspect and retighten all terminal screws.
        - **Contactors** — inspect contact faces for pitting, burning, or welding. Replace contactors showing more than 50% contact wear.
        - **Capacitors (run and start)** — test with a dedicated capacitor meter (LCR meter). Compare measured capacitance to the rated value printed on the capacitor body. Replace any capacitor reading more than **10% below rated µF** — weak capacitors cause hard starting and reduced fan or compressor motor life.
        - **Motor windings** — megger test compressor and fan motors (500 V DC) and record insulation resistance values.
        - **Cable and insulation condition** — look for rodent damage, abrasion, or brittle insulation.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Inspect Refrigerant Pipework Insulation">
        Inspect the full run of insulated refrigerant pipework (where accessible) from indoor to outdoor unit. Replace any sections showing:
        - Compression or flattening (restricts refrigerant flow)
        - Tears, holes, or missing sections (causes condensation and water damage)
        - UV degradation (crumbling or brittle foam on outdoor sections)

        Use correctly-rated closed-cell elastomeric foam insulation (Armaflex or equivalent) of a thickness appropriate for the pipe diameter and ambient conditions.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Lubricate Fan Motor Bearings (Serviceable Types)">
        On outdoor condenser fan motors fitted with grease nipples, apply the manufacturer's specified bearing grease. Rotate the fan shaft slowly by hand while applying to distribute lubricant evenly. Most modern fan motors use sealed-for-life bearings — do not attempt to grease these. If in doubt, refer to the motor data plate; the absence of a grease nipple indicates a sealed bearing.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Test Safety Controls and Auto-Restart Functions">
        Verify the operation of all protective controls:
        - **High pressure cut-out** — with a gauge set connected, simulate a high-pressure trip by closing the service valve momentarily (briefly and carefully). Confirm the compressor shuts down at the rated trip pressure and requires manual reset (or auto-resets after the correct delay for auto-reset models).
        - **Low pressure cut-out** — confirm the setting is within specification.
        - **Soft-starter / auto-restart** — confirm the unit restarts automatically after a simulated power interruption if configured to do so, and that restart occurs only after the anti-short-cycle timer has elapsed (typically 3 minutes).
      </Step>

      <Step title="Clean Drain Pan and Apply Biocide Treatment">
        Perform a thorough clean of the indoor unit drain pan:
        - Remove and clean the drain pan if it is removable; wipe down in place if not.
        - Apply a dedicated HVAC drain pan biocide treatment — spray-applied or drop-in tablet formulation.
        - Confirm the drain pan is completely dry before re-fitting a removable pan and before reinstalling the filter.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Update Service Records and Compliance Log">
        Complete all service documentation:
        - Enter all work carried out, readings taken, parts replaced, and any defects noted in the equipment service log.
        - Update the **F-gas equipment log** (where applicable) with refrigerant type, quantity in system, any leak check results, and any refrigerant added or recovered.
        - Issue a service report to the client noting any recommended remedial works with a severity classification and recommended timeframe.
        - Schedule and confirm the next maintenance visit date.
      </Step>
    </Steps>
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## VRF and Multi-Split Additional Requirements

VRF and multi-split systems introduce complexity beyond standard split systems due to the variable refrigerant flow control, branch selector architecture, and larger refrigerant charge volumes. Carry out the following additional tasks at each annual service, in addition to all standard tasks above.

<Accordion title="Refrigerant Charge Verification Across All Indoor Units">
  On VRF systems, refrigerant distribution to individual indoor units is managed by electronic expansion valves (EEVs) rather than a fixed-orifice device. As a result, suction superheat must be checked **at each indoor unit individually**, not just at the outdoor unit:

  1. Use the system controller's diagnostic mode (or a manufacturer-specific service tool) to read the EEV opening percentage and thermistor temperatures for each indoor unit.
  2. Calculate superheat at each unit: **suction pipe temperature − evaporating temperature**. Target: **5–8°C** per unit.
  3. Significant deviations (superheat greater than 12°C or less than 3°C at any unit) indicate an EEV fault, thermistor failure, or a refrigerant distribution problem in the branch circuit. Investigate before closing out the service.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Inspect Branch Selector Boxes (BSBs)">
  In heat-recovery VRF systems, branch selector boxes (also called branch circuit controllers or BS units) allow simultaneous heating and cooling in different zones by directing liquid or gas refrigerant as required. During the annual service:

  - Open each BSB enclosure and check for error codes on the onboard LED display or via the system controller.
  - Inspect solenoid valves within the BSB for leakage (oil staining at valve bodies).
  - Check refrigerant pipe connections at the BSB for oil staining or frost.
  - Confirm that zones assigned to heating and cooling respectively are operating correctly — a faulty BSB often causes comfort complaints in isolated zones while the rest of the system performs normally.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Update System Controller Firmware">
  VRF system controllers, outdoor unit control boards, and indoor unit PCBs may receive firmware updates from the manufacturer that address control logic bugs, improve efficiency algorithms, or expand compatibility. During the annual service:

  1. Check the current firmware version against the manufacturer's latest release (via the manufacturer's technician portal or service app).
  2. If updates are available, download to an approved service tool and apply per the manufacturer's update procedure — do not interrupt a firmware update once started.
  3. After updating, verify system operation by running a full system test cycle and confirming all indoor units receive correct commands from the outdoor unit.

  <Info>
    Firmware updates may reset certain configuration parameters to factory defaults. Before updating, document all custom settings (setpoint limits, operating mode restrictions, address assignments) so they can be restored after the update.
  </Info>
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Log Refrigerant Additions (F-Gas Compliance)">
  For systems containing fluorinated greenhouse gas refrigerants (HFCs such as R-410A, R-32, R-407C, or HFOs such as R-454B), adding refrigerant to the system is a **legally regulated activity** in most jurisdictions:

  - **EU F-Gas Regulation (EU 2024/573)** and **UK F-Gas Regulation** require that any addition of refrigerant to a system containing ≥5 tonnes CO₂ equivalent (tCO₂e) be carried out by a certified technician and entered in the equipment log within one month.
  - The equipment log entry must include: date, quantity added (in kg), refrigerant type, technician certificate number, and company registration.
  - Systems containing ≥5 tCO₂e must also be leak-checked at a frequency determined by charge size — typically every 3 or 12 months.
  - Retain all F-gas records for a minimum of **5 years** and make them available to the relevant authority on request.

  Failure to maintain F-gas records carries significant regulatory penalties and can invalidate building compliance certifications.
</Accordion>
