Air-cooled packaged air conditioning units — including rooftop units (RTUs) and ground-mounted packaged systems — are the most widely deployed type of commercial air conditioning equipment. They are self-contained: the compressor, condenser coil, condenser fans, evaporator coil, and supply air fan are all housed within a single cabinet installed on a roof curb, a concrete pad, or a structural support frame. Because the condenser section is permanently exposed to outdoor weather, UV radiation, and airborne contaminants, consistent preventive maintenance is essential to preserve efficiency, prevent premature corrosion, and ensure safe operation throughout the equipment's design life.

<Tip>
  Schedule your annual deep-clean and overhaul **before the summer cooling season begins** — typically late spring. An air-cooled condenser coil that is heavily fouled at the start of a hot summer will operate at elevated head pressure throughout the peak period, increasing compressor run hours, energy consumption, and wear. Early-season maintenance delivers the highest return.
</Tip>

## Maintenance Schedule

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Monthly">

    Monthly site visits should be carried out while the unit is in operation so live performance data can be collected. Allow 45–90 minutes per unit. Always follow your site's safe work procedure for working at height on rooftop equipment before commencing any inspection.

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Inspect Condenser Coil Fins">
        Visually inspect the outdoor condenser coil on all accessible faces. Look for accumulated dirt, cottonwood/seed debris, grease deposits (near kitchen exhaust), bent fins, and physical impact damage. A light deposit can be removed with a low-pressure water rinse (2–4 bar maximum to avoid fin damage) or with a purpose-formulated no-rinse coil cleaner spray. Note the percentage of fin face that is blocked — more than 20% fin blockage measurably increases discharge pressure and should be addressed before the next visit. Do not use a high-pressure washer at close range as this will flatten fins irreversibly.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check Condenser Fans">
        With the unit running, observe each condenser fan for:
        - **Rotation direction:** Airflow should be upward (exhaust to atmosphere) on most RTUs. Incorrect rotation (check after any power interruption or capacitor replacement) causes the unit to recirculate hot discharge air.
        - **Amp draw:** Record running current on each condenser fan motor and compare to nameplate FLA.
        - **Unusual noise:** Clicking, grinding, or intermittent noise indicates a failing bearing, loose blade, or debris ingestion.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check Refrigerant Pressures">
        Connect service manifold gauges (or read from the unit controller if equipped with pressure transducers) and record suction pressure and discharge pressure. Reference the manufacturer's performance curve at the current outdoor ambient temperature to determine expected design pressures. A discharge pressure elevated above the design curve at the measured ambient indicates a dirty condenser coil, recirculation of hot discharge air, or refrigerant overcharge. Log all readings with date, time, and ambient temperature.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Inspect the Refrigerant Sight Glass">
        Observe the liquid line sight glass for a minimum of 30 seconds under stable operating conditions. A clear, bubble-free view indicates an adequate refrigerant charge. Bubbles or flash indicate low charge or restriction in the liquid line. Check the moisture indicator — green indicates dry; yellow or beige indicates moisture contamination requiring investigation.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check Supply Air Temperature">
        Measure the discharge (supply) air temperature at the unit outlet using a calibrated thermometer. Compare to the design supply air temperature for the application (typically 13–16 °C for comfort cooling, or as designed for process applications). A rising supply air temperature at the same setpoint indicates degrading refrigerant circuit performance, reduced airflow, or a failing economiser damper admitting excess warm outdoor air.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Inspect Return Air Filters">
        Remove and inspect the return air filter(s). Record the pressure drop across the filter bank if a magnehelic gauge or differential pressure indicator is fitted. Replace filters when pressure drop exceeds the manufacturer's limit (typically 100–150 Pa for standard-efficiency panel filters). Examine filters for physical damage, bypass gaps, and any evidence of moisture or mould. In dusty environments, filters may require inspection every two weeks.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check Evaporator Coil for Ice Formation">
        Inspect the evaporator coil visually through the access panel (on a running unit, or immediately after a shutdown) for ice accumulation. Ice on the evaporator indicates one of two root causes: **low airflow** (dirty filter, blocked return grille, failed supply fan) or **low refrigerant charge** (low suction pressure). Never chip or scrape ice from the coil — isolate the unit in fan-only mode to defrost, then investigate the root cause before restarting cooling mode.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Verify Drain Pan and Condensate Drain">
        Inspect the evaporator drain pan for standing water, sludge, or biological growth. Pour a small quantity of clean water into the pan and confirm it drains freely within 30 seconds. A blocked condensate drain causes pan overflow, water damage to ceilings, and microbial growth on the coil and ductwork. Clear any blockage using a drain clearing tool or short burst of compressed air.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Inspect the Electrical Panel">
        Open the unit's electrical access panel with the unit running (using appropriate PPE and following safe working procedures for live panel inspection). Check that:
        - All fuses are intact and of the correct rating.
        - Contactors are pulling in and out cleanly without excessive noise or sparking.
        - Control board LED indicators are in their normal/run states — consult the manufacturer's controller manual for LED fault codes.
        - There are no loose wires visible at terminal blocks.

        Log any alarms or fault codes from the controller display.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Log Amp Draws for All Motors">
        Record the running current on all three phases of the compressor motor and each condenser fan motor using a calibrated clamp meter. Compare to nameplate FLA and to readings from previous visits. Trending current draw is one of the most reliable indicators of developing mechanical wear, refrigerant problems, or electrical deterioration. Note any phase imbalance greater than 2%.
      </Step>
    </Steps>

  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Annual">

    Annual maintenance is a full inspection and overhaul. Isolate and lock out the unit before beginning any work. For rooftop units, follow your site's working-at-height procedure and two-person rule where applicable. Plan a six to eight hour window per unit; larger RTUs with multiple refrigerant circuits may require more time.

    <Warning>
      **Isolate and lock out / tag out the unit at the main disconnect switch before accessing any panels for electrical work, coil cleaning, or mechanical inspection.** Condenser fans and supply air fans can restart automatically on some units without warning. Verify isolation with a non-contact voltage tester before touching any electrical component or placing hands near fan assemblies. Never rely on the unit's on/off switch alone as your sole means of isolation.
    </Warning>

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Deep Clean the Condenser Coil">
        Apply a purpose-formulated alkaline or acid coil cleaning chemical to all condenser coil faces using a low-pressure foaming sprayer. Allow the chemical to dwell for the time specified by the manufacturer (typically 5–15 minutes). Rinse thoroughly with clean water, working from the inside of the coil outward to flush debris away from the fin surface. After rinsing, inspect the full coil face and use a **fin comb** to straighten any bent aluminium fins — even partial fin blockage meaningfully reduces condenser capacity. Photograph before and after for the service record.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Deep Clean the Evaporator Coil">
        Remove the unit's indoor access panels to fully expose the evaporator coil. Apply an approved evaporator coil cleaner (self-rinsing type is suitable if good drainage is confirmed; otherwise rinse thoroughly). Inspect the coil face and fins for corrosion, particularly formicary (ant-nest) corrosion on copper tubes in the presence of formic acid from biological sources — small pinholes in tubes are a sign. Photograph any corrosion and include in the service report. Apply a biocide spray to the coil surface after cleaning to inhibit mould regrowth.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Perform Refrigerant Leak Check">
        Using a calibrated electronic refrigerant leak detector set to the manufacturer's recommended sensitivity level, systematically test all brazed joints (at the compressor, condenser coil headers, evaporator coil headers, and line set connections), all service valve cores, all Schrader port caps, and any pressure relief valve outlets. Move the probe slowly — at approximately 25–50 mm/sec — around each joint. Repair all detected leaks before proceeding with any refrigerant top-up. Re-test all repaired joints before closing panels. Document all findings.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Inspect and Re-Torque All Electrical Connections">
        With the unit fully isolated, remove all electrical panel covers. Inspect every terminal block, contactor lug, and bus bar connection for signs of overheating (discolouration, melted insulation, carbonisation), corrosion (white or green deposits on copper), or looseness. Re-torque all power-circuit connections to the manufacturer's torque specification using a calibrated torque screwdriver. Replace any contactors showing pitting or arcing on the main contacts — worn contacts cause intermittent faults and compressor short-cycling. Check overload relay trip settings against motor nameplate FLA and correct as needed.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check Crankcase Heater Operation">
        The compressor crankcase heater prevents refrigerant migration into the compressor sump oil during extended off periods. With the unit isolated, use a clamp meter to verify the crankcase heater is drawing current in accordance with its rated wattage. A failed crankcase heater is a common cause of compressor liquid slugging at startup, especially in cold climates or after extended shutdowns. Replace the heater element if it is found open-circuit.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Test Defrost Controls (Heat Pump Units)">
        On units configured as heat pumps, verify the defrost control board settings: defrost initiation mode (time-temperature or demand), defrost termination temperature setpoint, and maximum defrost duration. Initiate a forced defrost cycle using the board's test mode and verify the unit transitions correctly into defrost (reversing valve energises, outdoor fans stop, auxiliary heat engages if fitted) and terminates correctly on the temperature setpoint. Log the defrost cycle duration and the discharge pressure at defrost termination.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Inspect Roof Curb, Unit Base, and Structure for Corrosion">
        Inspect the roof curb rails, unit base frame, and any structural steel support frame for rust, section loss, or deteriorated protective coating. Pay particular attention to welded joints and areas where water can pond. Sand back surface rust to bare metal using a wire brush or angle grinder, apply a rust-converting primer, and finish with a compatible rust-inhibiting paint. Document any section loss that exceeds 20% of the original material thickness and report to the building structural engineer if deep corrosion is found.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Test All Safety Controls">
        Test each safety device individually and confirm correct trip behaviour:
        - **High-pressure cutout:** Verify trip setpoint matches the manufacturer's specification for the installed refrigerant (e.g., 4,150 kPa for R-410A). Confirm manual-reset or auto-reset behaviour matches the controller configuration.
        - **Low-pressure cutout:** Verify setpoint prevents evaporator freeze (typically set to the saturation pressure equivalent of 0 °C).
        - **Thermal overloads:** Confirm trip current settings on compressor and fan motor overloads match motor nameplate FLA. Manually trip and reset each overload to confirm mechanical function.

        Log all test results with measured values and pass/fail status.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Replace Air Filters">
        Replace all return air filters with new filters of the correct size, type, and efficiency rating regardless of visual condition. Confirm filter frame seals are intact and that there are no air bypass gaps around the filter media. Seal any bypass gaps with self-adhesive closed-cell foam tape. Record filter type and MERV/G rating for the service log.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Lubricate Fan Motors and Bearings">
        On units fitted with grease-lubricated (non-sealed, externally accessible) fan motor bearings or idler bearings, inject the correct type and quantity of grease per the bearing manufacturer's recommendation. Clean the grease nipple before and after greasing. Do not over-grease — consult the motor manufacturer's relubrication guide for grease quantity in grams per bearing at the applicable speed and load. Permanently sealed bearings require no lubrication; check for elevated temperature or vibration as an indicator of bearing life remaining.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Check Economiser Damper and Actuator (If Fitted)">
        Manually stroke the economiser (free-cooling) damper through its full range — fully closed to fully open — using the controller's actuator test mode or by manually operating the actuator. Confirm smooth operation with no binding or sticking. Check damper blade seals for deterioration; worn seals allow conditioned air to escape or unconditioned air to infiltrate. Inspect the actuator for signs of moisture ingress or seized operation. Calibrate the damper position to the controller's demand signal (0–10 V or 2–10 V typically) and verify the minimum outdoor air position matches the ventilation design.
      </Step>

      <Step title="Update Service Records">
        Compile all readings, inspection findings, test results, calibration data, and parts replaced into a formal annual service report. Update the unit's lifetime service log and attach all supporting documentation (photographs, leak test records, motor insulation test results). Provide a written recommendations list to the facility manager covering any items that require follow-up action before the next annual service, including refrigerant circuit concerns, structural corrosion requiring specialist assessment, or components approaching end of serviceable life.
      </Step>
    </Steps>

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Performance Reference Values

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Condenser Coil Approach" icon="sun">
    Discharge saturation temperature should be within **10–15 °C** of outdoor ambient. Higher values indicate a fouled coil, recirculation, or overcharge. Monitor monthly against ambient temperature.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Supply Air Temperature" icon="thermometer">
    Typical design supply air temperature is **13–16 °C** for comfort cooling. Confirm against your system's design documentation — process applications may vary significantly.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Filter Pressure Drop" icon="gauge-high">
    Replace filters at **100–150 Pa** differential pressure. Inspect every two weeks in high-dust environments and every month under normal conditions.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Motor Insulation" icon="bolt">
    All motor windings should read **> 100 MΩ** at 500 V DC. Trend annually; a declining reading approaching 10 MΩ warrants monitoring and planning for replacement.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Troubleshooting Common Issues

<Accordion title="High Discharge Pressure at Moderate Ambient Temperature">
  Inspect the condenser coil for fouling — a dirty coil is the most common cause. Check condenser fan operation: verify all fans are running and rotating in the correct direction. Check for discharge air recirculation (hot air blown back into the condenser inlet due to missing panels or baffles). Confirm refrigerant charge is not above nameplate weight.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Unit Short-Cycling (Starts and Stops Rapidly)">
  Short-cycling is usually caused by a safety control tripping. Read the fault code from the controller. Common causes include: high-pressure trip (dirty condenser coil), low-pressure trip (dirty air filter, low refrigerant charge), or thermostat over-satisfied too quickly (unit oversized for load). Check anti-recycle timer settings — minimum off-time of 3–5 minutes protects the compressor.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Increased Energy Consumption Without Change in Load">
  A gradual increase in energy use at the same load typically indicates condenser coil fouling, low refrigerant charge, or degraded compressor efficiency. Measure the system EER (kW cooling per kW electrical input) and compare to baseline commissioning data. Begin diagnosis by checking and cleaning the condenser coil, then investigate the refrigerant circuit.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Water Staining on Ceiling Below Rooftop Unit">
  Condensate drain pan overflow is the most likely cause. Inspect and clear the drain pan and drain line. Confirm the unit is installed level or with the correct slight slope toward the drain outlet. Also inspect the roof curb flashing and unit base gasket — deteriorated weatherproofing can allow rainwater ingress independent of the condensate system.
</Accordion>
